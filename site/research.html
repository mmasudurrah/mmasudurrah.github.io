Research

Learning-based autonomy for surgery and other high-stakes settings.

I develop reinforcement learning and foundation-model methods for systems that act reliably when conditions change and recognize when to hand control back to a person, with surgical and medical robotics as the driving application.

My work is organized around one question: when should an autonomous system act, and when should it hand control to a person? The same questions apply to robot learning and decision systems beyond surgery, wherever errors are costly.

Research program

Surgical autonomy that knows its limits.

The next stage of this work brings the three parts below together in surgical tasks: deciding how work is divided between a robot and the surgical team, supporting clinical decisions with evidence a clinician can check, learning policies that stay reliable when a procedure departs from plan, and evaluating systems against the disruptions and failures that occur during real procedures.
